05 / THE HIDDEN WATER COST

AI has a
water footprint.

Data centers generate heat while processing AI requests. Many cooling systems use water to keep servers from overheating—and that demand can put freshwater resources under pressure.

WATER USE / LARGE DATA CENTERUP TO
5M

↓≈ THE DAILY WATER USE OF A TOWN

A SCALE THAT IS HARD TO SEE

Cooling can draw
millions of gallons.

EESI reports that a large data center can consume up to 5 million gallons a day. A medium-sized facility may use about 110 million gallons a year just for cooling.

Different time scales: annual use vs. daily maximum. Figures are reported by EESI, not a direct like-for-like comparison.

01 / FRESHWATER PRESSURE
80%

Can evaporate.

Approximately 80% of water withdrawn by data centers—typically freshwater—evaporates. In drought-prone regions, this can intensify local water stress.

02 / INDIRECT WATER
211B

Gallons in 2023.

A federal report estimated U.S. data centers’ indirect water footprint from electricity use at roughly 211 billion gallons in 2023. Water is also used to generate the power that runs them.

03 / BETTER COOLING
−70%

Freshwater use.

EESI notes that closed-loop cooling systems can reduce freshwater use by up to 70%. Recycled water, liquid cooling, and renewable energy can also help reduce the footprint.

↗

The environmental trade-off: AI’s impact is not only about electricity or carbon emissions. Cooling and power generation also require water, so data centers should be designed around local water availability and more efficient cooling.

07 / BEHIND THE PAGE

How this
website was made.

I began by organizing the topic into a simple journey: first, what artificial intelligence means; then, where people use it; next, the risks; and finally, possible futures. I chose a bold editorial style inspired by contemporary digital design, using oversized typography, a limited palette, clear labels, and playful abstract graphics to make a complex subject feel approachable.

The page is built with semantic HTML for its sections and content, CSS for the responsive layout, colors, cards, and visual effects, and JavaScript for the category filters, expandable risk cards, and draggable timeline. I used interactive elements so visitors can explore information at their own pace instead of reading one long block of text. I tested the layout at different screen widths and made the controls keyboard accessible, with labels and visible states.

AI assisted me with brainstorming, organizing the explanation, and suggesting code. I reviewed the language to keep it accessible and checked factual claims against the linked sources. The timeline describes scenarios, not certain predictions. The final design and content were edited for this assignment, and I remain responsible for the work.
